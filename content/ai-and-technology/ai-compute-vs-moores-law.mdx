Moore's Law says the number of transistors on a chip doubles about every two years. For fifty years it set the pace of the computer industry.

The compute used to train the largest AI models has grown far faster. Epoch AI, a research group that tracks AI systems, estimates that training compute for notable models has grown 4 to 5 times every year since 2010. That is a doubling roughly every five to six months.

In 2010 the largest training run used about 5 × 10<sup>16</sup> operations. Epoch's estimate for the largest run published by September 2026 is 10<sup>27</sup>. That is an increase of about 18 billion times in 16 years.

Moore's Law over the same years would have delivered an increase of 256 times.

AlexNet, the image model that started the deep learning boom, used about 5 × 10<sup>17</sup> operations. Llama 3.1, released twelve years later, used about 80 million times as much.

## Where the extra compute comes from

Faster chips explain only part of the growth. Three other things did the rest.

1. **More chips per run.** Training now runs on tens of thousands of GPUs or TPUs working together.
2. **Longer runs.** Large models train for weeks or months.
3. **More money.** Epoch estimates the cost of frontier training runs has grown about 2 to 3 times a year.

Each of these has limits. Power supply, chip production and budgets will decide how long the trend can last. For now it shows no sign of matching the slower pace of Moore's Law.
